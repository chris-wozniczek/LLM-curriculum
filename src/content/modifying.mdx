## Changing a model without (much) training

Fine-tuning (module 11) changes weights by training. A whole community toolbox changes them with **arithmetic** instead: averaging models, subtracting directions, deleting layers or experts. It's cheap (minutes on a CPU), and it's where many of the "uncensored", "merged" and "pruned" repos on Hugging Face come from.

## Merges

A merge combines several fine-tunes of **the same base model** into one. They need identical architecture and tensor shapes, and in practice a shared base, so their weights are still "compatible".

| method | idea |
|---|---|
| linear / model soup | weighted average of the weights ([Wortsman et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.05482)) |
| SLERP | interpolate two models along a sphere instead of a straight line, which keeps the weight norms sensible |
| task arithmetic | take each fine-tune's difference from the base (its "task vector") and add them up |
| TIES | task arithmetic, but keep only the largest changes and resolve sign conflicts between models ([Yadav et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01708)) |
| DARE | randomly drop most of each task vector and rescale the rest before merging ([Yu et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03099)) |

The standard tool is [mergekit](https://github.com/arcee-ai/mergekit) ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.13257)), and its YAML config is the **merge recipe** from module 13:

```yaml
merge_method: ties
base_model: Qwen/Qwen3-8B-Base
models:
  - model: someone/qwen3-8b-coder      # illustrative names
    parameters: { weight: 0.6, density: 0.5 }
  - model: someone/qwen3-8b-writer
    parameters: { weight: 0.4, density: 0.5 }
dtype: bfloat16
```

Labs also merge their own checkpoints, for example averaging several RL runs. It's a regular tool, not just a hobbyist trick.

## Abliteration

[Arditi et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717) found that in many chat models, refusing is controlled by **one direction** in the residual stream (module 03). Find that direction, remove it from the weights, and the model mostly stops refusing. The rest of its behaviour changes little. That's **abliteration** (ablation + obliteration):

1. Run two sets of prompts through the model: ones it refuses and similar ones it answers.
2. At one layer, take the average hidden state of each set at the last prompt token. The difference between the two averages is the **refusal direction** `r` (normalized to length 1).
3. Remove `r` from every matrix that writes into the residual stream (embeddings, every `o_proj` and `down_proj`): `W ← W − r rᵀ W`. Now no layer can write anything along `r`.

That's plain linear algebra: no training, no gradients. Here it is on Qwen3-0.6B, measured with `labs/abliterate.py` (deliberately mild prompts: insults, fake doctor's notes, sneaking into a cinema):

What abliteration does **not** do: it doesn't add knowledge or skills. It often makes models slightly worse elsewhere (here: {Math.round(R.abliterated.harmless_top1_agree * 100)}% top-1 agreement with the original on harmless prompts), and it removes safety behaviour that exists for good reasons. Community abliterations usually follow up with a short fine-tune ("healing") to recover quality. Tools: [FailSpy's abliterator](https://github.com/FailSpy/abliterator), [Heretic](https://github.com/p-e-w/heretic), and the original paper's code.

## Steering

The same idea without editing weights: **add** a direction at inference time instead of removing one. Take the difference between activations for "talk about weddings" and neutral text, add a multiple of it to the residual stream during generation, and the model drifts toward weddings ([Turner et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10248)). It's used for tone, persona, or honesty experiments, and the strength is a dial you turn at runtime.

## Pruning

- **Layer pruning**: deeper layers are often surprisingly redundant. [Gromov et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17887) removed large fractions of later layers from Llama-2-70B with little loss on knowledge benchmarks, after a small healing fine-tune.
- **Expert pruning** (module 08): drop rarely used or low-impact MoE experts, as in REAP. The model gets smaller in total size, not faster per token.
- Both remove capabilities that your test set didn't cover. Evaluate on your own tasks (module 14).

## Upscaling and frankenmerges

The opposite: make a model **bigger** by stacking copies of its layers. SOLAR 10.7B did this properly ("depth up-scaling": two copies of a 32-layer model, overlapping layers removed, then continued pretraining; [Kim et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15166)). Community **frankenmerges** do it with mergekit's `passthrough` method, stacking layer ranges from one or two models without retraining. Some work surprisingly well, many just get slower. Measure before believing.
